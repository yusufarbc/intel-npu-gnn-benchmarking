This review synthesizes current empirical research on the interaction between deep learning compiler optimizations—specifically operator fusion—and Neural Processing Unit (NPU) performance, with an analytical lens focused on memory constraints and I/O complexity. Drawing on recent literature from 2021 onward, the review explores the tension between theoretical performance gains promised by operator fusion and the practical overhead that emerges when compilation, dispatch, and hardware fallback costs are accounted for—especially for small, latency-sensitive models deployed at the edge.

---

## Executive Summary

The memory wall—the growing disparity between processor speed and memory bandwidth—has fundamentally reshaped the optimization landscape for AI inference, shifting the bottleneck from arithmetic throughput (TOPS) to data movement. This transition is especially acute on Neural Processing Units (NPUs), where on-chip memory is severely constrained and off-chip DRAM access dominates energy and latency costs. Operator fusion, long considered a cornerstone graph optimization in compilers such as ONNX Runtime and OpenVINO, aims to reduce intermediate memory traffic by merging multiple layers into a single kernel launch. While fusion has consistently demonstrated robust gains for large convolutional architectures (e.g., ResNet-50), recent empirical studies reveal a more complex picture for small models, heterogeneous hardware, and unsupported operator types. Specifically, a **"Fusion Overhead Paradox"** has begun to emerge: for compact models like BERT-tiny or DistilGPT-2, the compilation cost, kernel dispatch latency, and the performance penalty of CPU fallbacks for unfused operators can exceed the actual inference time saved by fusion itself. Current research remains fragmented—few studies provide integrated telemetry of compilation overhead, memory peak, and end-to-end latency on the same NPU pipeline—leaving a critical evidence gap for compiler designers and edge AI practitioners.

## Thematic Synthesis

### Theme 1: The Evolution of the Memory Wall

The conventional RAM model of algorithmic analysis assumes uniform memory access cost, an assumption that collapses on modern AI accelerators. Wolters et al. (2024) provide a systematic demonstration that memory access now consumes orders of magnitude more energy and time than floating-point computation, particularly for large language model (LLM) inference where the weight matrices exceed on-chip SRAM capacity by a factor of 10–100× . Their survey of compute-in-memory (CIM) architectures positions the memory wall as the primary obstacle to efficient scaling, noting that the discontinuation of Moore’s law has further exacerbated this gap.

The roofline model remains the dominant analytical framework for characterizing this shift. Pagoda (2025), an energy-and-time roofline study on Jetson AGX Orin, extends the traditional compute-vs-bandwidth analysis with both a **time roofline** (execution latency as a function of arithmetic intensity) and a novel **energy roofline** that links memory access byte count directly to power consumption . The study empirically demonstrates that memory bandwidth limits inference latency for models with arithmetic intensity below approximately 30 FLOPs/byte on the Orin’s 204.8 GB/s DRAM, a threshold that encompasses virtually all compact vision and NLP models. Farrés García (2025) applies roofline analysis explicitly to NPU architectures, demonstrating that quantization’s benefit is not uniform but depends on whether the workload sits in the compute-bound or memory-bound region . For memory-bound convolutions in YOLOv3, INT8 quantization improved throughput by 2.1× solely by reducing data movement, not arithmetic cycles—directly implicating operator fusion as a complementary mechanism.

A key limitation of existing roofline work is its **static nature**: most studies analyze a single model at a fixed precision and batch size, failing to capture the dynamic interaction with graph optimizations. The component-based roofline for Huawei Ascend NPUs proposed by researchers at Nanjing University (2025) attempts to address this by decomposing the roofline into precision-transport combinations, but stops short of integrating compiler-level fusion decisions into the model .

### Theme 2: Efficacy of Operator Fusion

Operator fusion operates on a straightforward principle: merging adjacent computational layers avoids writing and reading intermediate feature maps to off-chip DRAM, reducing memory traffic and kernel launch overhead. ONNX Runtime’s documentation describes canonical patterns—Conv+BatchNorm, MatMul+Add, Conv+ReLU—as well as more aggressive attention fusion inspired by FlashAttention . The quantitative benefit on large CNNs is well-established: Niu et al.’s DNNFusion (PLDI 2021) demonstrated up to 8.8× more fusion opportunities compared to pattern-based frameworks and a 9.3× speedup across 15 models .

**When fusion succeeds—large CNNs and high arithmetic intensity.** The OptiFX framework (2024) targets the GPU domain but provides transferable insights. Its cost-based backtracking search over the fusion space achieved significant speedups on memory-bound CNN operators, demonstrating that fusion is most effective when (a) intermediate feature maps are large relative to on-chip buffer capacity and (b) the fused kernel achieves high arithmetic intensity . Optimus (2023) extends this to DNN accelerators, introducing a dedicated memory cost model for the scheduler. Across varying accelerator dataflows, Optimus reduced off-chip memory accesses by 17–75% and improved energy efficiency by 1.86×–3.66×, with the largest gains on lightweight models where feature maps dominate the memory footprint .

**When fusion fails—the overhead paradox.** The fusion story becomes more nuanced for small models and heterogeneous execution. Zhang et al.’s Unified Operator Fusion (UOF, 2025) provides the most direct empirical evidence of the overhead paradox. While UOF reduced ResNet-50 latency on an NPU from 120 ms to 32 ms (3.75× speedup), its ablation study revealed that removing the hardware-aware cost model caused **over-fusion**: on BERT-small, blindly applying all possible fusions resulted in a 15% *slowdown* compared to selective fusion, because the compile-time dispatch overhead and register pressure exceeded the memory bandwidth savings . Their cost model explicitly balances three factors: compute savings, data movement reduction, and kernel-launch latency—a tripartite trade-off that naive fusion strategies ignore.

Forge-UGC (2026), validated on Intel AI Boost NPU, extends this evidence with a dedicated metric: the **Fusion Gain Ratio** (inference speedup divided by compilation cost). Across six transformer families (125M to 8B parameters), Forge-UGC’s register-graph engine reduced NPU-CPU transitions by 42–65% and delivered 18.2–35.7% lower inference latency compared to OpenVINO and ONNX Runtime. Critically, its per-pass execution profiling revealed that the optimization passes themselves consumed significant time: graph node reduction of 14.2–21.9% was achieved, but the compilation overhead of the fusion pass was non-trivial, contributing to a 6.9–9.2× faster overall compilation primarily by avoiding redundant IR traversals .

For small, latency-critical models on edge devices, the empirical study by Cai et al. (2023) on operator-level DNN performance reports that framework-level fusion (TensorRT vs. PyTorch) produces inconsistent latency relationships between separated operators and operator blocks—fusion gains on a Jetson Nano can vanish entirely when the model’s operator graph is already sparse or when batch size is 1 .

### Theme 3: Compiler-Hardware Friction

The most significant threat to operator fusion’s effectiveness on NPUs is not algorithmic but systemic: **CPU fallbacks for unsupported operators**. A model that runs 80% on NPU and 20% on CPU can be slower than running entirely on CPU, due to the round-trip cost of memory synchronization across the heterogeneous boundary .

**NPU compiler limitations.** Intel’s OpenVINO documentation and community testing (2025) highlight a critical constraint: NPU compilers require **static, compile-time-known tensor shapes**. When models employ dynamic sequence lengths—common in all autoregressive language models—the NPU compiler fails with shape inference errors. A Hugging Face community analysis confirms that DistilGPT2 and LLaMA inference on Intel NPU "fails with compiler errors, typically related to missing upper bounds on tensor shapes," forcing a complete CPU fallback . A GitHub issue from October 2025 reports that an Intel 265K NPU running LLM inference is "1 to 2 orders of magnitude slower than CPU," directly attributable to unsupported dynamic shape operators triggering full CPU execution .

**The fallback penalty is non-linear.** Analysis of ConvNext on NPU+DSP architectures by SemiEngineering (2024) demonstrates that fallback solutions assuming 100% DSP utilization still achieve less than 1 inference per second for a 1600×928 input, while a unified GPNPU (general-purpose NPU) runs 28× faster on the same workload . The penalty arises from: (a) PCIe/DMA transfer of intermediate tensors between NPU local memory and CPU DRAM, (b) CPU kernel launch overhead for individual unfused operators, and (c) pipeline stalls while waiting for synchronization. Each of these factors multiplies for models with many unsupported operators, creating a compound overhead that can dominate end-to-end latency.

**OpenVINO’s heterogeneous execution model.** OpenVINO’s AUTO and HETERO modes partition computational graphs across devices, but current profiling shows that the partitioning is agnostic to the true cost of CPU fallback. Forge-UGC addresses this gap with a device-affinity scheduling phase that explicitly minimizes NPU-CPU transitions. Its linear-scan buffer allocation reduces peak buffer count by 30–48%, lowering the memory footprint of intermediate tensors that cross the NPU/CPU boundary .

## Research Gaps

1. **Dynamic Roofline Modeling for NPU Compiler Integration.** Current roofline models are static, analyzing fixed model architectures without incorporating the effect of compiler graph rewrites. No published work integrates operator fusion decisions as a first-class variable in the roofline framework—i.e., dynamically recomputing arithmetic intensity after fusion to predict the new performance ceiling. The component-based roofline on Ascend NPU represents a step toward precision-aware modeling but does not extend to fusion-induced changes in the memory access pattern .

2. **Telemetry and Observability for Edge NPU Inference.** There is a striking absence of open-source tools that provide fine-grained, per-layer telemetry of NPU memory bandwidth utilization, intermediate tensor residency, and CPU fallback transitions during live inference. Forge-UGC’s per-pass profiling and Compilation Efficiency Index are promising but remain bespoke to a single compiler. Standardized metrics—comparable to NVIDIA Nsight for GPUs—are lacking for Intel, Qualcomm, and Ascend NPUs.

3. **The "Fusion Overhead Paradox" for Tiny Models Remains Underexplored.** While UOF’s ablation study on BERT-small provides initial evidence of over-fusion slowdowns , no systematic study has mapped the parameter space where compilation and dispatch overhead exceeds fusion benefit. Variables such as model parameter count (< 10M vs. > 100M), operator count, batch size, and NPU driver latency remain uncharacterized. This gap is critical for TinyML and on-device inference where models are deliberately small.

4. **Predictive CPU Fallback Cost Models.** Current heterogeneous schedulers (OpenVINO AUTO, ONNX Runtime heterogeneous execution) partition graphs across NPU/CPU/GPU without a predictive model of the fallback penalty. A cost model that estimates the DMA transfer time, CPU kernel launch latency, and synchronization stall for each unsupported operator could enable *cost-conscious partitioning* that avoids pathological fallback configurations.

5. **Cross-Vendor NPU Fusion Rule Standardization.** Operator fusion rules are deeply vendor-specific: Intel NPU supports a different fusion pattern set than Qualcomm Hexagon or Huawei Ascend. There is no common taxonomy of supported fusions, forcing compiler developers to maintain per-backend rule sets (as noted in NxPU’s issue tracker ). Research on hardware-agnostic fusion representations (UOF’s IR) is a nascent step but lacks cross-vendor validation.

6. **End-to-End Energy Accounting of Fusion-Compilation Overhead.** Most fusion studies report inference energy savings but exclude the one-time compilation energy cost. For edge devices where models are loaded infrequently but compiled at load time, the amortization period of fusion-driven compilation overhead against per-inference savings is unknown. Pagoda’s energy roofline methodology could be extended to include compilation energy as part of total cost of ownership.
