# Operator Fusion on Neural Processing Units: A Literature Review on Memory-Wall Constraints, I/O Complexity, and Compiler-Hardware Friction

**Prepared for:** Master's Thesis in Software Engineering
**Review Period:** 2021–2026
**Review Type:** Thematic Synthesis with Empirical Focus

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The deployment of deep neural networks on Neural Processing Units at the edge represents one of the most active and contested domains in systems research. A consensus has emerged across the literature that the primary bottleneck in modern AI accelerator performance is no longer arithmetic throughput but memory bandwidth and I/O complexity — a structural condition formalised as the "memory wall." Several AI applications are increasingly bottlenecked by intra- and inter-chip data movement across accelerators rather than by compute, and this is not a new phenomenon; the term "memory wall" was coined as early as 1995 when Wulf and McKee predicted that the cost of memory access would dominate processor performance.

What is new in the 2021–2026 period is the collision of this long-standing hardware reality with two concurrent trends: the proliferation of edge NPUs in consumer SoCs (Intel Core Ultra, Qualcomm Snapdragon X, Apple M-series) and the adoption of graph-level compiler optimisation frameworks — principally ONNX Runtime and OpenVINO — as the standard deployment pathway. The intersection of these trends has produced an empirically observable phenomenon that this review terms the **Fusion Overhead Paradox**: for small models with low arithmetic intensity (notably BERT-tiny and similarly parameterised encoder architectures), the compilation overhead, graph-partition dispatch cost, and CPU-fallback penalties associated with operator fusion can exceed the actual model execution time, yielding a net negative performance outcome from what is theoretically a beneficial optimisation.

The literature reviewed here is drawn from peer-reviewed venues (ASPLOS, OSDI, ACM JETC, MDPI Electronics, IEEE Micro, Frontiers in Science) and from high-quality preprint sources. Empirical studies utilising physical telemetry — including latency, memory peak, power draw, and hardware utilisation counters — are prioritised over theoretical modelling alone. The following synthesis identifies three thematic pillars, surfaces a cross-cutting paradox, and concludes with a set of specific research gaps that remain unaddressed as of April 2026.

## 2. Thematic Synthesis

### Theme 1: The Evolution of the Memory Wall — From Compute-Bound to Memory-Bound AI Workloads

The framing of AI hardware performance through the Roofline Model, originally proposed by Williams, Waterman, and Patterson for multicore CPUs, has been extensively adapted to the NPU context in recent years. The model partitions workload performance into two regimes delineated by arithmetic intensity (AI), defined as the ratio of floating-point operations to bytes of memory traffic. Any algorithm with arithmetic intensity below the hardware's critical threshold will be bandwidth-bound and limited by peak memory bandwidth, regardless of how many compute units are provisioned.

This theoretical framework has gained renewed empirical urgency because of a structural asymmetry in hardware scaling. Modern accelerators see compute (FLOPS) scale up to 20× faster than memory bandwidth, causing a growing subset of AI workloads — particularly GEMV-dominated inference and small-batch generative decoding — to be throttled by memory rather than arithmetic. The consequence is that the "compute-bound" assumption underlying classical algorithm analysis via the RAM model becomes systematically invalid for a wide class of production inference tasks.

This divergence is quantified precisely by Gholami et al. (2024), who profile encoder and decoder Transformer architectures against this framework. Profiling results for BERT-Base, BERT-Large, and GPT-2 demonstrate that arithmetic intensity varies by orders of magnitude across model families: decoder models exhibit dramatically lower arithmetic intensity than encoder models due to their matrix-vector-type operations versus matrix-matrix operations, making decoder inference far more memory-constrained despite having lower total FLOPs.

The implication for NPU design is significant. The physical compute architecture itself can influence arithmetic intensity by providing larger on-chip caches, hardware support for spatial and temporal data reuse, or quantization support — meaning that arithmetic intensity is not solely a function of the workload but also of the hardware architecture executing it. This invalidates a common assumption in compiler toolchain design: that a fixed fusion strategy tuned to a workload's FLOP count will generalise across hardware platforms.

At the system architecture level, in most machine learning applications, DNN models and data are too large to fit on-chip, which means the majority of the model and data reside in DRAM. The movement of data to and from DRAM consumes significant time and energy in current AI hardware, including GPUs and specialised ML accelerators such as TPUs and NPUs. For edge NPUs, which typically have 32–40 MB of on-chip SRAM, this constraint is far more severe than for data-centre accelerators. Independent benchmark data from early 2026 confirms that sustained NPU inference on models exceeding 3 billion parameters rarely exceeds 15 tokens per second on flagship mobile processors, compared to 60–80 tokens per second at cloud inference endpoints on the same models.

This theme establishes the foundational premise for all subsequent analysis: the standard RAM-model assumption of uniform memory access cost is not merely imprecise for NPUs — it is categorically wrong. Any evaluation of operator fusion or graph optimisation that does not account for the memory hierarchy, on-chip SRAM capacity, and DRAM bandwidth ceiling of the target NPU will produce misleading conclusions.

### Theme 2: Efficacy of Operator Fusion — When It Helps and When It Hurts

Operator fusion is the primary graph-level optimisation deployed by frameworks such as ONNX Runtime, TVM, and OpenVINO. Its theoretical mechanism is straightforward: by combining multiple operators (e.g., Conv → BatchNorm → ReLU, or MatMul → GELU → LayerNorm) into a single kernel, intermediate activations can remain in fast on-chip SRAM rather than being written back to and re-read from DRAM between operator boundaries. This reduces effective I/O complexity and increases the arithmetic intensity of the fused kernel.

The empirical literature confirms that this mechanism works well in the conditions for which it was designed: large convolutional models with high arithmetic intensity and sufficient tensor sizes to amortise kernel launch overhead. Unified Operator Fusion reduced ResNet-50 latency from 120 ms to 32 ms on a mobile NPU, from 25 ms to 12 ms on a V100 GPU, and from 120 ms to 75 ms on an Intel Xeon CPU, while matching vendor-tuned library performance within 5–10%. These results are consistent with what the Roofline Model predicts: ResNet-50 is a compute-heavy model with high arithmetic intensity; fusion reduces data movement cost at operator boundaries and keeps the hardware in the compute-bound regime.

The picture changes substantially for small Transformer models — particularly those in the BERT-tiny family — for several interconnected reasons.

First, the per-operator execution time is small relative to the constant cost of kernel launch and synchronisation on NPU hardware. An NPU kernel dispatch involves DMA setup, memory barrier insertion, and driver-level scheduling, costs that are largely invariant to tensor size. When the execution time of a fused operator is on the order of tens of microseconds, the dispatch overhead can represent a non-trivial fraction or even the majority of wall-clock time.

Second, fusion strategies must be designed with device-specific cost models to avoid pathological outcomes. Removing the cost model from the unified fusion pipeline led to excessive fusion choices and degraded performance by up to 15%, demonstrating the importance of device-aware optimisation. This finding is critical: it shows that fusion without hardware-awareness is not a neutral operation — it is actively harmful.

Third, the heterogeneous operator support coverage of current NPUs means that not all fusion candidates can execute on the accelerator. Depending on which execution provider is used, it may not have full support for all operators in the model, and fallback to CPU ops can cause significant hits in performance speed. When a fused subgraph contains even one unsupported operator, the entire subgraph may be rejected by the NPU execution provider and routed to the CPU, effectively negating the I/O savings that motivated the fusion.

The Intel AIPC benchmarking study (arXiv 2409.14803) provides direct empirical evidence of this effect. For dot product operations, the CPU demonstrated the lowest latency among all tested platforms, suggesting that for sufficiently simple or small kernels, NPU dispatch overhead exceeds execution time. This is a concrete empirical instantiation of the Fusion Overhead Paradox: the NPU is slower than the CPU for this primitive precisely because the fixed cost of invoking the accelerator cannot be recovered by computational savings at small problem sizes.

The oneDNN Graph Compiler work (2024) extends this analysis to the attention mechanism. Resource-constrained edge devices rely on graph-fusion-based kernels — such as oneDNN for CPUs and FLAT for NPUs — to accelerate attention computations by fusing operators and retaining intermediate results on-chip, which reduces DRAM and off-chip memory access overhead. However, these fused attention kernels are architecturally specialised: they assume specific tensor layouts, fixed sequence lengths, and operator ordering that can conflict with the dynamic shapes emitted by general-purpose ONNX exporters, particularly for BERT-tiny's condensed encoder blocks.

A secondary and understudied dimension is compilation latency. The ONNX Runtime documentation explicitly distinguishes between online optimisation (applied at session initialisation, adding to startup latency) and offline optimisation (serialised to disk). Applying all optimisations each time a session is initiated can add overhead to the model startup time, especially for complex models, which can be critical in production scenarios where instance scaling speed under high load matters. For edge NPU deployments — where startup-to-first-inference latency is often the operative metric for interactive applications — this compilation overhead is a direct and measurable cost that the literature has not yet systematically quantified as an isolated variable.

### Theme 3: Compiler-Hardware Friction — CPU Fallbacks, NPU Driver Limitations, and OpenVINO Fragility

The third theme concerns the engineering gap between what compiler frameworks promise and what NPU hardware delivers in practice. This gap is most acutely visible in the domain of CPU fallbacks: the silent rerouting of unsupported subgraphs from the NPU execution provider to the CPU, which imposes both compute penalties (CPU is slower for matrix operations) and synchronisation costs (data must traverse the PCIe or UMA bus between devices).

The severity of this problem is articulated clearly in the industry literature. If 98% of the computation speeds through the NPU accelerator but the complex SoftMax final layer executes 100× or 1000× slower on the CPU, the slow CPU performance dominates the entire inference time — and this problem only worsens over time as ML models evolve to include new operator variants or network topologies not hardwired into the accelerator. This is Amdahl's Law operating in the context of heterogeneous execution: the unsupported minority of operators determines the achievable speedup ceiling of the majority.

The ACM review on edge LLMs (2025) frames this as a structural characteristic of current NPU architectures: NPUs are optimised for a limited set of neural network operators, making them incompatible with many modern LLM architectures. This often forces fallback strategies like CPU-NPU co-processing, which can offset performance gains. Additionally, the rapid evolution of neural architectures further complicates matters, as NPUs struggle to keep up with model diversity and complexity, requiring extensive adaptations or failing to execute some models entirely.

Intel's OpenVINO — the primary compiler and runtime stack targeting Intel Core Ultra NPUs — exemplifies this fragility empirically. OpenVINO release notes between 2023 and 2025 document a persistent stream of model-specific failures: TensorFlow Object Detection models converted via the OVC tool exhibit poor performance on CPU, GPU, and NPU devices; ONNX model conversion fails when openvino-tokenizers is installed; and compilation of models performing weight quantisation fails with segmentation faults on Intel Core Ultra 200V processors. These are not theoretical failure modes — they are documented regressions in production toolchain releases.

User-reported experience on Intel Community forums corroborates this pattern. Not all model architectures are fully optimised for the NPU, and some layers or operations may still fall back to the CPU if unsupported; the recommended diagnostic procedure is to use the benchmark_app with the -d NPU flag and examine output logs for fallback messages or partial execution warnings. The fact that end-users must manually audit execution logs to determine whether their model is actually running on the NPU — rather than this being guaranteed by the compiler — is itself indicative of the maturity gap in current NPU toolchains.

The Parallax framework (Tang et al., 2025) represents a significant attempt to address this problem at the framework level. Existing frameworks handle fallbacks poorly, leaving CPU cores idle and causing high latency and memory spikes. Parallax partitions the computation DAG to expose parallelism, employs branch-aware memory management with dedicated arenas and buffer reuse to reduce runtime footprint, and uses an adaptive scheduler that executes branches according to device memory constraints — achieving up to 46% latency reduction, 26.5% average memory overhead, and up to 30% energy savings compared with state-of-the-art frameworks.

The IANUS architecture (Heo et al., ASPLOS 2024) approaches the same problem from the hardware-software co-design angle. NPUs are often limited by memory-bound operations even when high-bandwidth memory is utilised; to address this, IANUS proposes an NPU-PIM architecture that routes memory-bound operations to processing-in-memory and compute-bound operations to the NPU's systolic array, providing the benefit of both domain-specific accelerator and domain-specific memory to effectively support a broad range of arithmetic intensities in LLMs.

The broader edge AI deployment literature confirms that compiler-hardware friction is not an Intel-specific problem but a systemic characteristic of the field. Although NAS-based compression, joint pruning-quantisation strategies, and mixed-precision inference represent advanced techniques, their adoption in embedded environments remains limited by practical constraints such as hardware-specific bit-width support, memory-bandwidth restrictions, and heterogeneous toolchains across NPUs, Edge-TPUs, and microcontrollers.

## 4. The Fusion Overhead Paradox: A Synthesis

Drawing across all three themes, this review proposes a formal characterisation of the Fusion Overhead Paradox as follows. Let $T_{exec}$ denote the model execution time on the NPU in the absence of compilation or dispatch overhead, and let $T_{overhead}$ denote the total cost of graph compilation, kernel dispatch, CPU-NPU synchronisation barriers, and any CPU fallback execution. The net benefit of deploying on the NPU (versus native CPU inference) is:

$$\Delta T = T_{CPU} - (T_{exec} + T_{overhead})$$

For large models (e.g., ResNet-50, BERT-Large), $T_{exec}$ is large and $T_{overhead}$ is amortised, so $\Delta T > 0$ substantially. For small models (e.g., BERT-tiny, MobileNetV3-small), $T_{exec}$ is small; if the model contains unsupported operators that force CPU fallbacks, $T_{overhead}$ may be comparable to or exceed $T_{CPU}$, yielding $\Delta T \leq 0$ — the NPU deployment is counterproductive.

The empirical evidence for this phenomenon is currently fragmented across multiple studies, none of which have directly measured all components of $T_{overhead}$ in isolation on a production NPU with a controlled set of small models. This constitutes the most important research gap in the field.

## 5. Research Gaps

The following gaps are identified through systematic analysis of the reviewed literature. They are ordered from most to least critical for a Master's-level software engineering thesis contribution.

**Gap 1: Absence of dynamic roofline modelling for edge NPUs.** Current roofline analyses (Gholami et al., 2024; benchmarking studies) apply static rooflines derived from manufacturer specifications. These specifications report peak TOPS at INT8 under ideal, sustained conditions. Real-world sustained inference rarely achieves peak specifications because they do not account for the memory starvation that occurs the moment a model's weight tensor exceeds on-chip SRAM capacity, and NPUs frequently thermal-throttle within 90 seconds of continuous operation, dropping to approximately 60% of peak performance. No published study has constructed a dynamic roofline model that captures the time-varying effective bandwidth and compute ceiling of a production edge NPU under thermal and memory-pressure conditions.

**Gap 2: No systematic isolation of compilation-phase overhead as an independent variable.** The reviewed literature treats graph optimisation overhead either qualitatively (mentioning it exists) or as an offline pre-processing step. There is no published study that measures the compilation latency of ONNX Runtime's graph optimisation passes (constant folding, operator fusion, layout optimisation) and OpenVINO's model compilation pipeline as a function of model complexity for sub-100M parameter models on edge NPUs. This overhead is particularly relevant for deployment scenarios where models must be recompiled at runtime (e.g., due to dynamic input shapes or model updates), and its interaction with the Fusion Overhead Paradox has not been empirically characterised.

**Gap 3: Inadequate telemetry tooling for edge NPU inference.** The diagnostic pathway for identifying CPU fallbacks in OpenVINO requires manual log inspection. No standardised profiling API exists across Intel NPU, Qualcomm QNN, and Apple Neural Engine that provides per-operator execution placement (NPU vs. CPU), memory bandwidth consumed, and dispatch latency as machine-readable telemetry. Deployment toolchains require extensive manual adjustments due to immature toolchains and limited profiling capabilities. Without such tooling, reproducible empirical research on fusion overhead is structurally difficult.

**Gap 4: Lack of empirical benchmarks on sub-100M parameter NLP models on production NPUs.** The available empirical literature focuses either on large language models (LLaMA, GPT) or computer vision models (ResNet, YOLO). BERT-tiny (4.4M parameters), DistilBERT (66M), and MobileBERT (25M) — the class of models most likely to expose the Fusion Overhead Paradox — have not been systematically benchmarked on Intel Core Ultra NPUs or Qualcomm Snapdragon X NPUs with full operator-placement telemetry reported. The ONNX Runtime documentation notes that BERT model optimisation sometimes fails due to graph pattern matching issues, but no systematic empirical study has quantified the frequency or latency impact of these failures on current hardware.

**Gap 5: Absence of hardware-agnostic fusion policy optimisation frameworks.** The cost-model approach in UOF (Liu et al., 2024) represents a promising direction, but it requires per-device calibration. No published framework provides an automated, hardware-agnostic mechanism to determine the optimal fusion granularity for a given model-hardware pair at deployment time, accounting for both I/O complexity reduction and NPU dispatch overhead. The interaction between quantisation precision (FP32, FP16, INT8, INT4), fusion depth, and CPU fallback probability on edge NPUs has not been characterised as a joint optimisation problem.

**Gap 6: No longitudinal study of fallback rate evolution with model architecture updates.** As the Quadric analysis observes, NPU operator coverage is fixed at tape-out while model architectures evolve rapidly. The rate at which newer model architectures (e.g., models with FlashAttention-2, grouped-query attention, or SwiGLU activations) trigger CPU fallbacks on existing consumer NPU hardware has not been empirically tracked over time. Such a longitudinal study would provide quantitative evidence for the claim that fallback penalties worsen with each new model generation, directly informing procurement and deployment lifecycle decisions.

## 6. Conclusion

The literature reviewed here establishes a coherent and technically rigorous picture of the current state of NPU inference optimisation. The memory wall is empirically confirmed as the primary performance bottleneck for AI accelerators across scales, from data-centre TPUs to laptop-class NPUs. Operator fusion, when applied with hardware-aware cost modelling and adequate operator coverage, delivers substantial latency and energy benefits for large, compute-regular models. However, for small, operator-diverse models deployed on edge NPUs with immature driver ecosystems, fusion overhead and CPU fallback penalties represent a measurable and undercharacterised performance liability — the Fusion Overhead Paradox.

The research gaps identified above delineate a precise and tractable empirical agenda for a Master's thesis contribution: designing a telemetry-instrumented benchmarking methodology that isolates compilation overhead, dispatch latency, and fallback cost for sub-100M parameter NLP models on production edge NPUs under realistic thermal and memory-pressure conditions. Such a study would provide the first systematic empirical characterisation of the conditions under which NPU deployment is net-beneficial versus net-harmful for small model inference — a question with direct practical significance for the growing ecosystem of on-device AI applications.
